2K or 4K: The Bits-Per-Pixel Trap Behind "Bigger Is Better"

Two encodes, same bitrate setting, different resolutions. The 4K one gets half the bits per pixel. This is arithmetically forced and almost never mentioned, and it is why some people report that 4K looks worse than the 2K they rejected.

14 November 2026 · x264 encodes measured with ffprobe

The question "should I go 2K or 4K" is usually answered with a resolution argument. It is not a resolution argument. It is a budget argument, and the budget is bits per pixel.

The arithmetic nobody puts in the table

Pixels scale with the square of the linear dimension. Going from 2K (1440×2160, 3.11 Mpx) to 4K (2160×3240, 7.00 Mpx) multiplies pixel count by 2.25. If you keep the same bitrate — which is what happens when the encoder target does not change — each pixel gets 2.25× fewer bits to describe itself.

Measured on a real 1080×1620 source, re-encoded with x264:

ResolutionTargetFile size Bits/pixel
2K (1440×2160)4M91,670 B0.2358
2K8M163,512 B0.4206
2K16M311,668 B0.8016
4K (2160×3240)4M112,027 B0.1281
4K8M195,656 B0.2237
4K16M371,061 B0.4242

Read across the 8M row: 2K gets 0.4206 bits per pixel, 4K gets 0.2237. Not exactly half, because the encoder is not perfectly efficient at reallocating, but close. Read across the diagonal instead and the useful insight appears: 4K at 16M (0.4242 bpp) is essentially the same bit density as 2K at 8M (0.4206 bpp) — and costs 2.27× the file size for 2.25× the pixels.

The rule that falls out. To keep quality constant when you double the linear resolution, you must roughly quadruple the bitrate. Going 2K→4K at the same bitrate is not "the same quality at higher resolution" — it is a deliberate quality cut in exchange for pixel count.

When 4K is genuinely the right answer

Three cases, and they are narrower than the marketing implies:

  1. The output is text or UI. Glyph edges are the highest-frequency content a video can contain, and they are also the most compressible-when-clean. A 4K screen recording of a document downscaled to 1080p for delivery loses legibility that no bitrate recovers, because the loss is spatial, not a compression artifact.
  2. The viewer will crop or zoom. If the frame is a canvas for later reframing, extra pixels are working space. This is a production argument, not a delivery one — the audience never sees the 4K master.
  3. The display is actually 4K and the viewing distance is close. At normal TV distance, human acuity stops resolving detail well before 4K on most content. On a monitor at arm's length, it does not.

When 2K wins outright

The common case: a source that was never 4K. If you start from 1080p or below and target 4K, you are spending 2.25× the bits describing detail that was interpolated, not captured. In the interpolation measurements, Lanczos at 4× retained 1.3% of the source's high-frequency energy versus 21.5% at 2×. You are paying full 4K bitrate for content that carries roughly one-seventeenth the detail information of a native 2× upscale.

That is the mechanism behind "the 4K looks worse." The upscale invented softness, then the encoder was given half the bits to describe the soft result, and soft content with too few bits develops banding and block edges that were not in the 2K version.

How to decide per file, in one pass

  1. What is the source's true resolution? Not the container's. Check the actual detail — if the source was upscaled already, its real resolution is lower than its pixel count.
  2. What is the delivery target? Match it, do not exceed it. Delivering 4K to a 1080p player costs bandwidth and returns nothing.
  3. Compute the budget. Target bits-per-pixel for archival is roughly 0.4–0.8; for delivery, 0.1–0.2 is normal. Multiply by pixel count to get your bitrate, rather than picking a bitrate and accepting whatever bpp falls out.
  4. Check the soft-content case. If the content is smooth gradients (sky, skin, walls), low bitrate produces banding before it produces blockiness. That is a different failure and needs a different fix — higher bit depth, not higher bitrate.

Reproducing it

for res in "1440:2160" "2160:3240"; do
  for br in 4M 8M 16M; do
    ffmpeg -i src.png -vf scale=$res -b:v $br -c:v libx264 out.jpg
    # bpp = filesize*8 / (w*h)
  done
done
