2K or 4K: The Bits-Per-Pixel Trap Behind "Bigger Is Better"

Two encodes, same bitrate setting, different resolutions. The 4K one gets half the bits per pixel. This is arithmetically forced and almost never mentioned, and it is why some people report that 4K looks worse than the 2K they rejected.

14 November 2026, updated 17 November 2026 · x264 encodes measured with ffmpeg and ffprobe

The question "should I go 2K or 4K" is usually answered with a resolution argument. It is not a resolution argument. It is a budget argument, and the budget is bits per pixel.

The arithmetic nobody puts in the table

Pixels scale with the square of the linear dimension. Going from 2K (1440×2160, 3.11 Mpx) to 4K (2160×3240, 7.00 Mpx) multiplies pixel count by 2.25. If you keep the same bitrate — which is what happens when the encoder target does not change — each pixel gets 2.25× fewer bits to describe itself.

Measured on a real 1080×1620 source, re-encoded with x264:

Read across the 8M row: 2K gets 0.4206 bits per pixel, 4K gets 0.2237. Not exactly half, because the encoder is not perfectly efficient at reallocating, but close. Read across the diagonal instead and the useful insight appears: 4K at 16M (0.4242 bpp) is essentially the same bit density as 2K at 8M (0.4206 bpp) — and costs 2.27× the file size for 2.25× the pixels.

The rule that falls out. To keep quality constant when you double the linear resolution, you must raise the bitrate roughly with the pixel count. The version of this rule usually repeated says quadruple — that figure turns out to be wrong, and the next section measures the real number on both upscaled and native-4K content.

Testing the quadruple-bitrate rule

The rule above — quadruple the bitrate to hold quality across a linear doubling — is repeated everywhere and I had written it on this page without measuring it. Then I measured it. It is wrong in both directions, and the way it is wrong is more useful than the rule.

The experiment is an equal-quality ladder. Both deliverables are encoded with the same encoder (libx264, -preset slow, yuv420p, single-threaded for determinism) across CRF 18–32, and each encode is scored with SSIM against its own pre-encode frame. That last detail is the whole point: same-CRF comparisons across resolutions are not comparisons, because CRF is a quality target, not a bitrate target — raising the pixel count at fixed CRF lets the encoder spend more bits per frame and land at a different quality point. So I solved for equal SSIM instead: for each quality level, interpolate the byte count of the 4K stream and the 2K stream that hit the same SSIM, and divide.

# ladder, both sizes, same encoder settings
for res in 1440:2160 2160:3240; do
  for crf in 18 20 22 24 26 28 30 32; do
    ffmpeg -i ref.png -c:v libx264 -preset slow -crf $crf \
           -pix_fmt yuv420p -threads 1 out_${res}_${crf}.mp4
    # SSIM of encode against ref.png, then interpolate log(bytes) at target SSIM
  done
done

Run it twice on two different kinds of source, because the answer depends on the source more than on the encoder:

Two readings. First, nothing here is 4×. The most expensive case measured, native 4K detail, needs 2.16× the bytes for equal encoder fidelity — roughly the pixel ratio, not double it. The cheapest case, upscaled content with no high-frequency information above 2K, needs 1.25×. A frame upscaled from 1080p is mostly smooth at 4K, and smooth content is nearly free to encode; the extra 3.89 Mpx cost about a quarter more bytes, not 4×.

Second, the direction of the error flips with content. On the upscale source, same-CRF overstates the cost by 34% (1.68× claimed versus 1.25× real) because the 4K encode is quietly buying 0.003–0.007 more SSIM. On native 4K content, same-CRF slightly understates it at high CRF, where the SSIM gap turns negative and the 4K encode is actually the worse one at the same setting. The same-CRF shortcut is not conservative in either direction; it is just unmoored.

What replaces the rule. The number that matters is not "quadruple the bitrate" but "what does the extra pixel count actually contain?" Dividing by pixel count is only valid when the pixels carry detail. Measured directly: 4K at CRF 24 (SSIM 0.98814) lands between 2K at CRF 22 (0.98664) and CRF 20 (0.98888), and costs 1.23× the bytes of a 2K encode at that same quality. So on upscaled content the honest price of the bigger frame is about one CRF step of quality plus a quarter more bytes — not 4× the bitrate.

The other side of the ledger: 4K does not degrade faster

If 4K carried hidden fragility, re-encoding would expose it. Three generations at CRF 23, each generation decoded to PNG before the next encode:

DeliverableGen 1 SSIMGen 2 Gen 3Loss g1→g3Bytes g3 ÷ g1
2K (1440×2160)0.985270.98116 0.97798−0.007290.937×
4K (2160×3240)0.989160.98606 0.98348−0.005680.942×

The 4K stream loses less SSIM per generation, not more — 0.0057 versus 0.0073 over two re-encodes. Both streams shrink with each generation, which is the tell: the encoder is deleting detail it no longer has to describe, and the file gets smaller as the content gets smoother. The 4K chain shrinks slightly less, consistent with having more detail left to discard. This is a negative result for the intuition that a larger frame accumulates damage faster, and I am recording it as measured rather than arguing the page's thesis around it.

One honest limit on all of the above: SSIM saturates on smooth content and stops discriminating. On a pure gradient source every encode scores 0.9985–0.9992 across six CRF steps and both resolutions — a range of 0.0007, which is noise, and the byte ratios computed inside that window (1.15× to 1.70×) are not meaningful. The ratios in the table above come from sources with enough structure to keep SSIM off its ceiling. Encoder fidelity is also not perceived sharpness: a 4K frame that scores the same SSIM as a 2K frame can still look sharper to a viewer who is close enough to resolve it, and SSIM says nothing about that.

When 4K is genuinely the right answer

Three cases, and they are narrower than the marketing implies:

  1. The output is text or UI. Glyph edges are the highest-frequency content a video can contain, and they are also the most compressible-when-clean. A 4K screen recording of a document downscaled to 1080p for delivery loses legibility that no bitrate recovers, because the loss is spatial, not a compression artifact.
  2. The viewer will crop or zoom. If the frame is a canvas for later reframing, extra pixels are working space. This is a production argument, not a delivery one — the audience never sees the 4K master.
  3. The display is actually 4K and the viewing distance is close. At normal TV distance, human acuity stops resolving detail well before 4K on most content. On a monitor at arm's length, it does not.

When 2K wins outright

The common case: a source that was never 4K. If you start from 1080p or below and target 4K, you are spending 2.25× the bits describing detail that was interpolated, not captured. In the interpolation measurements, Lanczos at 4× retained 1.3% of the source's high-frequency energy versus 21.5% at 2×. You are paying full 4K bitrate for content that carries roughly one-seventeenth the detail information of a native 2× upscale.

That is the mechanism behind "the 4K looks worse." The upscale invented softness, then the encoder was given half the bits to describe the soft result, and soft content with too few bits develops banding and block edges that were not in the 2K version.

The cascade myth: does upscaling in two steps beat one?

Every upscaling forum eventually produces this advice: do not jump 4× in one step, go 2× twice, because "the algorithm has less work to do at each stage." It sounds mechanically plausible, which is why it survives. It is also false, and the reason it survives is that almost nobody measures it.

Six paths from the same 100px source back to a 400px ground truth. HF% is high-frequency energy relative to the ground truth (the same 2-D FFT radial mask used throughout these notes). coh is a metric I added for this test: high-frequency phase coherence against the ground truth, which asks whether the high frequencies present point in the same direction as the real ones. Phase coherence of 1.0 means the detail is real; 0.0 means the filter produced energy that has nothing to do with the original content.

coh = <Re(F_up * conj(F_gt))>_hf_band
      / ( |F_up|_hf_band * |F_gt|_hf_band )

On the synthetic Siemens star, 4× from 100px:

Read the second row against the first. The cascade bought 0.0001 SSIM and 0.2 points of HF, and actually lost phase coherence — 0.132 versus 0.139. Two steps are indistinguishable from one step. Three steps are indistinguishable from two. The overshoot detour to 8× and back cost roughly four times the compute for a result identical to one step in every column.

The same test on a real photo crop tells the same story with a different absolute scale (400px ground truth from a 100px source): single 4× gives PSNR 27.31 / HF 21.1% / coh 0.355; cascade 2×+2× gives 27.32 / 21.3% / 0.354. At 2× instead of 4× — 200px to 400px — direct was 13.69 dB at 21.5% HF and a 300px cascade was 13.68 dB at 21.3%. There is no scale factor at which cascading helps. Interpolation is a linear operator; two linear operators of the same total scale compose to the same thing, and the only difference is rounding noise in the 8-bit intermediate.

Why the myth feels true. Cascading does change one thing: it stores an intermediate file. If that intermediate is written as JPEG between steps, the second step is now sharpening compression artifacts, and the result looks different. Different is not better — it is the same operation plus a generation of loss.

What invented detail costs the encoder

The fourth row above is the interesting one. "Round it up with nearest-neighbour, then smooth" is a real technique — it is how pixel-art upscaling keeps edges crisp. On a star pattern it produces 7× the high-frequency energy of a single Lanczos pass: 9.3% versus 1.3%.

It also produces half the phase coherence — 0.076 versus 0.139. The extra energy is not recovered detail. It is the square staircase of nearest-neighbour, low-passed just enough to look like texture. PSNR barely notices (11.62, the highest in the table) because PSNR is computed per pixel against a blurred reference and staircase artifacts are locally small. The p99 gradient, which measures edge overshoot, jumps from 13.7% to 24.3%.

Now the part that connects back to bits per pixel. Invented high-frequency energy is not free at encode time. I generated three 4K frames from the same 1080×1620 source — one direct upscale, one 2K→4K cascade, one nearest-mid — and encoded each with x264 at CRF 18, 23, 28 and at a fixed 8M target:

Two clean readings. First, the cascade row is within 0.2% of the direct row at every CRF setting — negative at CRF 23 and 28, which is encoder noise, not a real saving. The 0.5% loss of HF energy that the cascade introduces shows up as a 0.5% smaller file, and as 0.10 dB lower PSNR against its own pre-encode frame. Nothing was gained; a little was lost.

Second, the nearest-mid row costs +14% / +11% / +6% more bytes at CRF 18 / 23 / 28 to encode the same content, while its fidelity against its own source frame drops (PSNR 46.69 versus 47.47 at CRF 18). The encoder is spending real bits describing a staircase that was never in the photograph. This is the bits-per-pixel trap of this page's first section, arriving from an unexpected direction: it is not only resolution that eats the budget, it is any high-frequency content you manufacture before encoding. Staircase detail has the worst possible cost-to-value ratio because it is maximally expensive to encode and entirely fictional.

Practical conclusion, in one line: upscale once, at the final size, and never pre-sharpen a frame you are about to encode. If the frame needs sharpening, sharpen it as the last step of the delivery pipeline or not at all — and expect it to cost 5–15% more bytes for the halo it adds.

Does the codec change the 4K penalty?

Everything above measured one encoder. If the extra cost of a 4K frame were a property of pixels, it should be the same number for every codec — the pixel count is the same, after all. It is not. The penalty turns out to be almost entirely a property of how well the codec predicts, and the three mainstream encoders disagree by a factor of four.

The method is an equal-quality ladder, run separately for each codec and each size. Eight frames, all-intra (-g 1) so no frame borrows quality from its neighbours, single threaded for determinism. Each encode is scored with SSIM against the exact pre-encode frames, decoded to RGB on both sides so a yuv/tv-range mismatch cannot flatter the score. Then I interpolate log(bytes) at a common SSIM target and divide.

# one ladder per codec, per resolution, all-intra
for codec in libx264 libx265 libsvtav1; do
  for res in 1440:2160 2160:3240; do
    for crf in $LADDER; do
      ffmpeg -framerate 25 -i seq/f%03d.png -vf scale=out_range=tv \
        -c:v $codec -crf $crf -g 1 -threads 1 out.mp4
    done
  done
done

# SSIM in RGB space, so both sides are the same interpretation
ffmpeg -i out.mp4 -framerate 25 -i seq/f%03d.png \
  -lavfi '[0:v]format=rgb24[d];[1:v]format=rgb24[r];[d][r]ssim' -f null -

The source is the same 1080×1620 photograph upscaled to 2K (1440×2160, 3.11 Mpx) and 4K (2160×3240, 7.00 Mpx) — the exact case where "the 4K looks worse" is reported, because the 4K frame is 2.25× the pixels carrying detail the source never had. Bit-identical repeat encodes were confirmed by md5 before any of this was trusted.

Read the fourth column. At identical measured quality, x264 charges 1.291× the bytes for 2.25× the pixels. x265 charges 1.100×. AV1 charges 1.044× — a 4.4% increase in file size for a 125% increase in pixel count. The pixel ratio is 2.25×, so x264 pays 57% of it, x265 49%, AV1 46%.

The "4K is expensive" effect is a codec-era artefact. It is real and large on H.264, which is what most of the advice was written against. On AV1 it is nearly gone. If you are still delivering H.264, you are paying 1.29× for the bigger frame; switching the delivery codec to AV1 removes almost the entire penalty before you touch a single encoder setting.

Same bitrate, different resolution: which one actually looks better?

The byte ratio answers "what does 4K cost". The question people actually have is the reverse: I have a fixed bitrate — should I spend it on 2K or 4K? That is a different experiment, so I ran it: identical target bitrates, both resolutions, same codec, SSIM against each one's own source.

ContentCodecBitrate 2K SSIM4K SSIMGap
Smooth (upscaled)x2648M0.951560.94231−0.00925

DetailedAV124M0.980270.96230−0.01797

Every gap is negative: at a fixed bitrate the 2K encode always scores higher, because SSIM is per-pixel fidelity and the 2K frame has fewer pixels to spend its bits on. Watch the size of the gap instead. On smooth upscaled content, x264's 4K frame is 0.0093 SSIM behind — real but modest. On detailed native-4K content at 24M the x264 gap is 0.065, seven times larger, and at 12M it is 0.121. The same bitrate that gives a comfortable 0.946 on the 2K frame gives 0.881 on the 4K frame.

Then invert it: how much extra bitrate does the 4K frame need to climb back to the 2K frame's score? On the smooth source at 8M, x264 needs 11.1M (1.39×), x265 10.4M (1.30×), AV1 10.7M (1.34×). Note that this exceeds the 1.29×/1.10×/1.04× byte ratio from the ladder — the two numbers are not interchangeable. The ladder holds SSIM fixed and asks what 4K costs; this question holds the 2K bitrate fixed and asks what 4K needs to catch up, and catching up costs more than the steady-state ratio because SSIM is saturating near the top of each ladder.

The detailed source produces the most useful negative result in this section: at 12M, the x264 4K frame needs 28.2M to match the 2K frame's 0.895 — 2.35× the bitrate, which is less than the 4.0× pixel ratio but far more than the 1.29× the ladder predicted. And AV1's 4K frame never reaches the 2K frame's 0.974 at any bitrate I tested, including 48M. On genuinely detailed content, the AV1 4K encode is not catching the AV1 2K encode by adding bits at these levels. That is not a codec defect — it is SSIM being per-pixel: the 2K frame is compared against a 2K source that has already thrown away the 4K detail, so it starts closer to its target.

What this means for the decision. The bitrate penalty for 4K is a real measurement, not a myth, and it is smallest on AV1. But SSIM is the wrong tool for choosing 2K versus 4K, because it scores fidelity to a reference, and a 4K delivery is usually chosen precisely to show detail the 2K reference cannot contain. Use the byte ratio from the first table to budget, and use a viewing test — not an SSIM column — to decide whether the extra pixels are visible.

The viewing test, finally measured

The previous section ended by admitting SSIM is the wrong tool for choosing between 2K and 4K, and that the honest way to decide is a viewing test. That is a convenient place to stop, so I did not stop there. The viewing test is measurable: encode both arms, display each one at the size you will actually ship to, and score against the truth for that display size. This section is that experiment, and it produces the clearest rule on this page.

The trap in every 2K-versus-4K comparison is that each arm gets scored against a different reference. Score the 4K file against 4K truth and it loses to a 2K file scored against 2K truth, because the 2K source has already discarded the detail the metric would have penalised. So the experiment has to be built the other way round: one reference per display size, both arms projected down to it.

Setup

Two synthetic 4K truths, 2160×3240, both 72 frames of a slow half-pixel-per-frame pan so the encoder has real inter-frame prediction to work with:

  • detail — noise band-limited to 0.485 cycles/px, i.e. energy right up to the 4K Nyquist limit. Hard geometric edges (90px and 70px bars), one large disc.
  • soft — the same generator, band-limited instead to 0.215 cycles/px, which is the Nyquist limit of the 2K grid. Nothing in this source exists above 2K resolution, by construction.

From each truth I derive three references — 4K (the truth itself), 2K (1440×2160) and 1080 (1080×1620) — all by Lanczos. Then two deliverables per bitrate: the 4K encode, and a 2K encode made from the 2K reference so neither arm pays for an extra resample it does not need. Everything is libx264 -preset slow, yuv420p, -g 25, single-threaded, CBR pinned with -maxrate and -bufsize 2× so the target actually binds.

First, does the target bind?

Equal bitrate target is not equal file size, and the difference is worth reporting because it is larger than most people assume. Across thirteen matched rungs on the detail content, the 4K file came out at 0.88–1.00× the 2K file — mean 0.938×. On the soft content, 0.97–1.02×, mean 0.995×. So a 4K encode at the same target is systematically a little smaller, which is x264's rate control undershooting when the content is hard and the frame is large. Both arms land within 12% of each other, which is close enough that "same bitrate" is a fair description — but it means bits-per-pixel is doing the real work, and bpp differs across the two arms by the full 2.25× pixel ratio:

Detail content needs 1.26 bpp at 4K to look decent; soft content at the same 8M target gets 1.14 bpp at 4K and is already near transparent on a 4K screen (SSIM 0.975), while detail at the same 1.26 bpp only reaches 0.833. Same bits, same pixel count, and the soft file looks far better — which is the point. This is the same conclusion as the bits-per-pixel trap in the first table, arrived at from the opposite direction: content, not resolution, sets the bpp you need.

The measurement that matters: equal file size

Nobody ships "the same bitrate at two resolutions". They ship a file. So the decision is made at equal bytes, and the question becomes: at a fixed delivery size, which arm looks better on the screen this file will be shown on. I interpolated SSIM linearly in bytes across the ladder for both arms and differenced them. Positive means the 4K file wins.

Three things fall out, and only the first one was what I expected.

1. On a 4K screen, the 4K file wins at every size, by a lot. On detail content the gap widens monotonically with budget — +0.041 at 2MB to +0.186 at 5.5MB — because that is the only arm with anything above 2K Nyquist to spend the bits on. This is the case that justifies 4K delivery and it is not a close call.

2. On a 2K screen the 2K file wins until roughly 4MB, then the 4K file takes over — barely. The crossover is at 3.95MB on detail content, and the margin either side of it is a rounding error: +0.001 at 4MB, +0.010 at 5MB. So below ~4MB, encoding at 4K and letting the player downscale is strictly worse than encoding at 2K. Above ~4MB it is level, and you are paying 2.25× the pixels for nothing visible. The practical reading: if your delivery target is a 2K screen, 4K encoding has no quality case at any size — only a forward-compatibility case.

3. On a 1080 screen the 2K file wins until 6.6MB. This is the result I did not expect and it is the most useful one. Even at 5.5MB, where the 4K file wins at 2K display, it still loses at 1080 by −0.003. The reason is chain length, not resolution: the 1080 comparison asks the 4K arm to do 2160→1080 in one Lanczos pass after decoding a 4K frame, and the 2K arm to do 1440→1080, a 0.75× downscale that barely resamples at all. Shorter chain wins. This is the same effect the cascade section found for upscaling, running in reverse.

The soft-content result is a negative result, and it is the interesting one

On soft — content with literally nothing above 2K resolution — the 4K file should be pure overhead. It is not quite: it crosses over at 4.31MB and wins above that, by margins of 0.001 to 0.002. Those margins are noise-level and I would not ship a decision on them. But why it wins is worth one paragraph, because it exposes exactly what SSIM rewards.

I measured the spectrum of each arm against the truth in the band above 2K Nyquist (0.215 cycles/px), using phase coherence to separate real detail from invented detail — coherence 1.0 means the high frequencies are the right ones in the right places, 0.0 means energetic but unrelated.

The soft 4K encode carries 2.29× the high-frequency energy of its own source at a coherence of 0.60. It is manufacturing texture that does not exist, and SSIM gives it credit for it because per-pixel similarity rewards local contrast. That is the same failure mode described earlier in this page under "what invented detail costs" — nearest-neighbour mid-steps earned 7× the HF energy at half the coherence and the highest PSNR in the table. Same mechanism, appearing here without anyone asking for it: a 4K encoder given a 2K-worth signal will spend bits inventing, because that is what minimises its RD cost.

The rule. Choose by display size, not by source resolution and not by pixel count. 4K delivery earns its bytes only on a 4K display, and there it earns them at every budget tested. On a 2K display the crossover is ~4MB, above which the 4K file wins by less than 0.01 SSIM — you are paying for forward compatibility, not quality. On a 1080 display the 4K file does not win at all inside the tested range, because the extra downscale costs more than the extra resolution returns.

This is the number the old "quadruple the bitrate" rule was trying to express, and now it can be measured directly: interpolate each arm's ladder to a common SSIM target and divide the bytes.

The real cost of 4K delivery at equal perceived quality is 1.18–1.31×. Not 2.25× (the pixel ratio), not 4× (the folklore), and not 1.0× either. It is close to the pixel ratio's square root, which makes sense once you notice that SSIM is dominated by the low-frequency structure both arms encode identically — you are paying 2.25× the pixels to improve a metric that only partly cares about the pixels you added.

Two cautions on reading that table. It is a matched-quality ratio, so it understates what a fixed-budget 4K delivery costs: if you cannot raise the bitrate, the 4K file takes the full 2.25× bpp penalty, which is what the first table on this page measures. And it depends on the display size, because the 2K arm's own score depends on the display size. Quote the display size with the number or the number means nothing.

soft232.82 MB4.66 MB1.65×0.98790.9936+0.0057
soft262.20 MB3.60 MB1.64×0.98040.9901+0.0097

Read the detail rows: the 4K export costs 1.36–1.48× the bytes and is worse at CRF 20 and 23 on a 2K screen (−0.0117 and −0.0038). At CRF 26 it is better by 0.0095. So on genuinely detailed 2K-source content, a 4K export buys nothing you can see on a 2K display and costs 40% more — the upscale invented detail, and encoding that invented detail costs bits. The soft rows are more interesting still: the 4K export wins by up to +0.0097 at less than the 2.25× pixel penalty (1.64×), because the encoder is inventing smooth texture that SSIM likes. Both are consistent with the coherence table above.

The honest summary: exporting 4K from a 2K master is a bet on where the file will be displayed. It pays on a 4K display and costs 1.4–1.65× to get there. It does not pay on a 2K display, where the shorter encode chain wins outright at low CRF. If you cannot name the display, encode the resolution the master actually has.

Reproducing it

# 72-frame truths at 2160x3240, then the 2K and 1080 references
python3 - <<'PY'
import numpy as np, os
from PIL import Image
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261119)
H, W = 3240, 2160
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(np.float32)
def bandlimit(n, c):
    F = np.fft.rfft2(n)
    r = np.sqrt(np.fft.fftfreq(H)[:,None]**2 + np.fft.rfftfreq(W)[None,:]**2)
    F[r > c] = 0
    return np.fft.irfft2(F, s=(H, W)).astype(np.float32)
def build(cut, name):
    base = 0.45 + 0.12*np.sin(2*np.pi*xx/(W*0.7)) + 0.10*np.cos(2*np.pi*yy/(H*0.55))
    t = bandlimit(rng.standard_normal((H,W)).astype(np.float32), cut)
    t /= t.std()
    edges = ((xx % 90) < 45)*0.10 - ((yy % 70) < 35)*0.08
    disc = (((xx-W*.5)**2 + (yy-H*.42)**2) < (W*.18)**2).astype(np.float32)
    img = np.clip(base + 0.055*t + edges + 0.09*disc, 0, 1)
    im = Image.fromarray((img*255).astype(np.uint8))
    for i in range(72):
        c = im.crop((0,0,2160-int(i*.5),3240-int(i*.5)))
        for d, s in [(f'seq_{name}',(2160,3240)), (f'ref2k_{name}',(1440,2160)),
                     (f'ref1080_{name}',(1080,1620))]:
            os.makedirs(d, exist_ok=True)
            c.resize(s, Image.LANCZOS).save(f'{d}/f{i:03d}.png')
build(0.485, 'detail')   # energy to 4K Nyquist
build(0.215, 'soft')     # nothing above 2K Nyquist, by construction
PY

          [1:v]format=rgb24,settb=AVTB[r];[d][r]ssim' -f null - 2>&1 | grep 'All:'

Two implementation notes that cost me an afternoon. First, settb=AVTB on both branches of the SSIM filter graph — without it ffmpeg warns that the timebases do not match and the per-frame pairing silently drifts, which moves the score by hundredths. Second, do not use -v error when scoring: the SSIM summary is printed at info level, so -v error hides the number you are looking for and the filter reports success with no output. I spent longer than I will admit chasing a parser bug that was a logging flag.

If you would rather not build the ladder, the same decision can be made by exporting one file at each size from your own master and comparing them at 100% on the display you are targeting. That is the test this section quantifies; the numbers above just say what to expect before you run it, and the expectation is that the answer turns almost entirely on screen size rather than on the footage. The upscaler this page is published alongside exposes both 2K and 4K output targets, which makes the comparison cheap to run on your own file.

Reproducing the codec comparison

# equal-quality ladder -> interpolate log(bytes) at matched SSIM
python3 - <<'PY'
import json, math
rows = json.load(open('ladder.json'))
by = {}
for r in rows: by.setdefault((r['codec'], r['res']), []).append(r)
for k in by: by[k].sort(key=lambda r: r['ssim'])
def interp(c, t, target):
    p = by[(c, t)]
    lo = [x for x in p if x['ssim'] <= target][-1]
    hi = [x for x in p if x['ssim'] >= target][0]
    f = (target - lo['ssim']) / (hi['ssim'] - lo['ssim'])
    return math.exp(math.log(lo['bytes']) + f*(math.log(hi['bytes']) - math.log(lo['bytes'])))
for c in ('x264', 'x265', 'av1'):
    b2, b4 = interp(c, '2k', 0.945), interp(c, '4k', 0.945)
    print(f'{c}: 4K/2K = {b4/b2:.3f}x')
PY

Two details decide whether the result reproduces. All-intra is not optional: at the same CRF the all-intra pair costs 1.847× while a GOP-25 pair costs 1.764×, because the 4K encode gets more inter-prediction help per frame — a 4.5% swing in the headline number from one flag. Use all-intra, or at minimum hold the GOP length fixed across both resolutions. And the ladder must be wide enough that both resolutions overlap around the target SSIM; my first attempt used a ladder that ran off the top of the 2K curve, and the interpolation silently returned nothing for every codec.

If you want the short version to act on: budget with the 1.29×/1.10×/1.04× table, remember that the 2K frame will always win the per-pixel score at a fixed bitrate, and settle the 2K-or-4K question with your eyes at the real viewing distance. The resolution ceiling that matters here is the source's, not the container's — and if the source never had the detail, no codec and no bitrate will invent it.

How to decide per file, in one pass

  1. What is the source's true resolution? Not the container's. Check the actual detail — if the source was upscaled already, its real resolution is lower than its pixel count.
  2. What is the delivery target? Match it, do not exceed it. Delivering 4K to a 1080p player costs bandwidth and returns nothing.
  3. Compute the budget. Target bits-per-pixel for archival is roughly 0.4–0.8; for delivery, 0.1–0.2 is normal. Multiply by pixel count to get your bitrate, rather than picking a bitrate and accepting whatever bpp falls out.
  4. Check the soft-content case. If the content is smooth gradients (sky, skin, walls), low bitrate produces banding before it produces blockiness. That is a different failure and needs a different fix — higher bit depth, not higher bitrate.

Reproducing it

    # bpp = filesize*8 / (w*h)
  done
